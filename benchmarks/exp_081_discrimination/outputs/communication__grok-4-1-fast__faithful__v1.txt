# Internal Communication as Organizational Infrastructure: Why Communication Satisfaction is the Lowest-Rated Workplace Factor Despite Having the Largest Impact on Retention, Satisfaction, and Productivity — and What the Non-Desk Worker Gap Reveals About Systemic Failures

## Executive Summary

Internal communication functions as the foundational infrastructure of modern organizations, transmitting vision, strategy, feedback, and support. Yet, the 2025 International Employee Communication Impact Study by Staffbase and YouGov (N=3,574 employees across Australia, Austria, Germany, Switzerland, UK, and US) reveals a stark paradox: communication satisfaction ranks lowest among workplace factors at 42-43%, trailing coworker relationships (76%), vacation/time off (71%), and manager support (59%). Despite this, it exerts the largest influence on retention (63% of employees cite it as a leaving factor; 76-point "very likely to stay" swing from excellent to poor communication), job satisfaction (64-point swing from very clear to very unclear vision), and productivity (63% report some/great impact). The non-desk worker gap—consistently 15-20 percentage points worse across metrics—exposes systemic failures in channel access, leadership reach, and inclusion, particularly for frontline staff. This analysis grounds all claims in the study's data.

## Communication Satisfaction: Consistently the Lowest-Rated Workplace Factor

The study identifies communication quality (42% satisfied) and communication amount (43% satisfied) as the lowest-rated factors among key workplace elements. In contrast:

- Coworker relationships: 76% satisfied (highest).
- Vacation/time off: 71% satisfied.
- Manager support: 59% satisfied.

This positions communication as a chronic underperformer, even as employees rate interpersonal and benefits-related factors highly. Non-desk workers amplify this dissatisfaction: only 9% are "very satisfied" with communication (vs. 14% desk-based), with total satisfaction at 29% (vs. 47% desk-based). Ratings of communication as "excellent/very good" stand at 28% for non-desk (with 38% "fair/poor") compared to 48% for desk-based.

The low satisfaction persists across sub-dimensions. Only 20% find the company's vision and strategy "very clear" (36% "somewhat clear," 7% "very unclear"). Just 23% feel well-informed about organizational changes (39% "not really/not at all informed"). Leadership communication fares poorly: 26% say leaders address concerns poorly/not at all (34% among non-desk). These figures underscore communication's infrastructural role—ubiquitous yet undervalued—while highlighting its failure to meet basic expectations.

## The Disproportionate Impact: Retention, Satisfaction, and Productivity

Despite low satisfaction, communication's downstream effects are outsized, rivaling or exceeding other factors. On **retention**, 33% of employees cite poor communication as a *major* factor for leaving, and 30% as a *minor* factor (total: 63%). In Germany, this rises to 41% major and 29% minor. Communication quality drives a 56-point swing in loyalty: 76% with "excellent" communication are "very likely" to stay, vs. 20% with "poor" communication.

For **job satisfaction**, clarity yields dramatic variance. A "very clear" vision correlates with 89% job satisfaction; "very unclear" drops to 25% (64-point swing). Change communication shows similar leverage: 88% happiness among those "very well informed," vs. 36% "not at all informed." Weekly+ senior leadership communications boost happiness to 77%, vs. 41% for those who "never" receive them.

**Productivity and motivation** are directly tied: 63% report communication has "some" or "great" productivity impact; 67% for work motivation; 65% for understanding vision/mission. "Easy to understand" communications result in 78% rating overall communication as "excellent/very good," vs. 3% when "not communicated effectively."

Crisis communication, rated "excellent/good" by 52%, reveals further leverage: 72% rate digital screens highest, yet 36% experience gaps, with only 49% feeling supported (38% non-desk). These metrics confirm communication's infrastructural primacy—low investment yields high leverage losses.

## The Non-Desk Worker Gap: Evidence of Systemic Failures

Non-desk workers (e.g., frontline, field-based) consistently lag 15-20 percentage points behind desk-based peers, revealing infrastructural inequities in access and inclusion.

- **Overall satisfaction**: 9% "very satisfied" (vs. 14%); 29% total satisfied (vs. 47%); 28% "excellent/very good" (vs. 48%).
- **Change communication**: 45% "not really/not at all informed" (vs. 36% desk-based).
- **Manager information**: 48% "well/very well informed" by managers (vs. 65%).
- **Senior leadership reach**: 12% non-desk "never" receive senior comms (21% in UK).
- **Feedback loops**: Only 12% say feedback during change is "yes" considered (vs. 19% desk-based); 28% say "never" considered. Overall, 24% feel excluded from change communication.
- **Crisis support**: 38% feel supported (vs. implied higher for desk-based in 49% overall).
- **Leadership concerns**: 34% say addressed poorly/not at all (vs. 26% overall).

Curiously, non-desk workers report less loneliness ("never lonely": 43% vs. 32% desk-based; 10% always/often lonely overall), yet employer efforts to foster connections rate only 20% "very good." This gap implicates channel biases: primary sources are email/memos (51%), immediate supervisor (47%), and intranet (39%)—tools skewed toward desk access. Employee apps (15% primary use) build trust to 60% among users, but adoption lags. Non-desk exclusion signals systemic failures: digital/structural barriers prevent equitable infrastructure deployment.

## Trust, Channels, and Feedback: Root Causes of Breakdowns

Trust hierarchies expose mismatches: immediate supervisors lead at 57%, followed by intranet (51%), email/memos (50%), newsletters (44%), and apps (41%; 60% for users). Social media is distrusted by 31%. This reliance on supervisor-centric channels falters for non-desk workers (48% well-informed by managers vs. 65% desk-based).

Feedback mechanisms fail hardest during change: desk-based 19% "yes" considered; non-desk 12%. Vision clarity (20% "very clear") and change info (23% well-informed) remain low, with non-desk penalties persisting.

The study notes no data on specific interventions beyond channels, but patterns suggest overreliance on desk-optimized tools neglects non-desk realities.

## Implications: Communication as Neglected Infrastructure

Communication's low satisfaction (42-43%) belies its outsized role—63% turnover linkage, 56-point retention swing, 64-point satisfaction swing—positioning it as organizational "infrastructure" akin to IT or facilities. Neglect here cascades: poor channels erode trust (e.g., 41% app baseline), motivation (67% impacted), and productivity (63%).

The non-desk gap (15-20pp deficits) reveals systemic failures: channel inaccessibility, leadership silos (12-21% never reached), and feedback voids (28% never considered). Desk-biased tools (email 51%, intranet 39%) exclude  frontline workers, widening divides. Loneliness data (non-desk less affected) hints at relational strengths untapped by formal comms. Organizations must prioritize inclusive infrastructure—e.g., apps/digital screens (72% crisis efficacy)—to unlock communication's leverage.

The study provides no prescriptive solutions or longitudinal data, limiting causal inferences to correlations shown.

## Conclusion

Communication satisfaction's rock-bottom ranking (42-43%) masks its infrastructural dominance: driving 63% of turnover considerations, massive satisfaction swings (89% vs. 25%), and 63-67% impacts on productivity/motivation. The non-desk gap—9% vs. 14% very satisfied, 45% vs. 36% uninformed—diagnoses systemic inequities in reach and inclusion. Addressing this unlocks retention (76% vs. 20% stay likelihood) and satisfaction. As the study's central tension affirms, investing in equitable communication yields disproportionate returns.
